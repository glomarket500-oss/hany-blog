《芳綸纖維：最薄嘅選擇，同埋佢唔防彈》Aramid Fibre: The Thinnest Choice, and It Won't Stop a Bullet

2026年10月8日Oct 8, 2026 產品回憶Product Notes 芳綸纖維Aramid Fibre

我哋賣芳綸殼，第一句就要講一句趕客嘅話：呢個殼唔防彈。

唔係謙虛，亦唔係法務部門叫我咁寫。係因為「芳綸」呢兩個字，喺大部分人腦裡面就直接連去防彈背心。如果我唔喺第一段講清楚，你買返去總有一日會覺得我騙你。

我係做廠出身，做過電子、家電、五金，二十六年。我唔係材料學家。但我有個習慣：每次有人同我講一種材料幾咁厲害，我都會問三條問題——呢個數字係點量出嚟？喺乜嘢條件下成立？幾時會唔成立？ 芳綸係我問得最多嘅一種材料，因為最多人吹。

呢篇文我會由分子講到成本，講佢點解係最薄嘅選擇，亦講佢邊度唔得。全部係我自己揀料時諗嘅嘢。你可以不同意，但唔會捉到我講錯數。

一、芳綸究竟係乜：一條唔肯摺嘅分子鏈

芳綸（aramid）係「芳香族聚醯胺」嘅簡稱。呢個名好悶，但拆開就明：芳香族＝分子骨架係苯環，硬、唔易扭；聚醯胺＝靠醯胺鍵（-CO-NH-）一節一節串起嚟，即係同尼龍同一個家族，只不過尼龍嘅鏈可以自由摺疊、好似一條煮軟嘅意粉，而芳綸嘅鏈係硬直嘅一條棍。

呢個分別聽落好學術，但佢就係一切嘅起點。你用嚟做殼嘅係對位芳綸（para-aramid），即係苯環同醯胺鍵一個接一個、向同一個方向排成直線，化學名叫 poly(p-phenylene terephthalamide)，簡稱 PPTA。你聽過嘅 Kevlar、Twaron 都係呢一家。另外有一款間位芳綸（meta-aramid，例如 Nomex），分子鏈係打橫岔開嘅，唔硬，但耐熱、阻燃——消防衣用嗰種。同樣叫「芳綸」，兩種性格，唔好混。

製造過程都幾特別。對位芳綸唔溶於一般溶劑，要溶喺濃硫酸裡面，形成一種「液晶態」溶液——即係話，分子鏈未紡成絲之前，已經自自然然排得幾整齊。工程師將呢種溶液由噴絲板挤出，經過一小段空氣層（呢個叫乾噴濕紡），再入凝固浴。空氣層呢一小段好關鍵：纖維會被拉長，分子鏈沿住纖維軸方向拉直、排列高度一致。結果就係：拉力沿住纖維方向傳，非常好；打橫或受壓，就即刻差。

具體數字，我用公開資料嘅範圍講，唔會扮到好精確：對位芳綸密度大約 1.44 g/cm³；拉力強度 大約 3 GPa 級數（換成紡織業嘅講法，每丹尼爾可以承受 20 克以上嘅拉力）；模數（即係有幾「硬頸」）大約 70 至 110 GPa，視乎型號。杜邦當年賣 Kevlar 嘅著名講法係：「同重量計，拉力強度大約係鋼嘅五倍。」呢句係佢哋嘅比較，唔係我嘅發明，我照引用。

但同一份資料仲有幾行細字，好多人唔睇：

  • 壓縮強度遠低於拉力強度。你拉佢，佢好強；你壓佢，纖維會扭結（kink）。所以芳綸係「受拉」材料，唔係「受壓」材料。
  • 紫外線會令佢變黃、降強度。所以佢一定要被封喺樹脂裡面，唔可以裸露。
  • 佢會吸濕。幾 percent 咁上下，唔算多，但足以令你要用樹脂封兩面。
  • 佢唔會熔。受熱到某個溫度（大約 500 °C 以上）會直接分解，唔會先變成一灘液體。
  • 同樹脂嘅黏合天生唔好，所以布料出廠前通常要做表面處理，呢個又要成本。

睇到呢度你應該明白：芳綸唔係「無敵材料」，佢係一種性格非常鮮明嘅材料——一條方向拉得住千斤，另外一個方向就一般。你要用佢，就要接受佢嘅脾氣，唔係硬套一個「高級」標籤上去。

二、由一束絲到一塊殼：丹尼爾、織法、克重

好多人以為「芳綸殼」係將纖維倒落模具就成。唔係。實際流程係：纖維紡成紗 → 織成布 → 布浸樹脂（或者直接用預浸布 prepreg）→ 多層疊起 → 熱壓固化 → 用 CNC 修邊開孔。每一格都影響你手上嗰塊嘢。

丹尼爾（denier）：一束絲有幾粗

丹尼爾係紡織業嘅基本單位，定義好簡單：9,000 米長嘅紗有幾重（克）。1,500D 就係 9,000 米重 1,500 克。數字越大，紗越粗。

呢個數字對你有咩影響？三樣：

  • 織紋有幾明顯。粗紗（例如 1500D）織出嚟嘅紋路立體、份量感重，睇落「厚料」；幼紗（例如 600D）織出嚟細密、平滑，遠睇接近一塊素色板。
  • 手感。幼紗摸落細緻，粗紗摸落有明顯嘅纖維起伏——有人叫「乾爽嘅纖維感」。
  • 耐磨同剛性。同一織法下，粗紗單位面積纖維多，耐磨好啲、硬啲，但亦重啲、厚啲。

手機殼行業常用嘅係 1000D 同 1500D。我唔會同你講「1500D 一定好過 1000D」，因為呢個係取捨：你要立體紋路，就要接受佢重少少同表面起落大啲；你要細緻平滑，就要接受耐磨同硬度犧牲。冇免費。

「3K twill」係乜，同埋佢其實係借嚟嘅詞

「K」係指一束紗裡面有幾多條單絲：3K = 一束 3,000 條。呢個講法本來係碳纖維行業嘅標準語言（碳纖最常講 3K、12K）。芳綸行業傳統上係用丹尼爾講，唔係用 K 講。所以當你見到「3K twill weave」寫喺一個芳綸殼上面，嚴格嚟講佢係借用咗碳纖嘅詞彙去形容表面織紋。呢個唔算錯，但你要知道：佢唔係一個可以拿去實驗室對照嘅規格，佢係一種外觀描述。

「twill」即係斜紋。斜紋織法每條紗跨過兩條、再壓住兩條，形成對角階梯紋——就係嗰種斜斜地反光嘅紋路。相比平紋（plain weave，一上一下），斜紋嘅布身軟啲、貼服啲，可以做到更貼合曲面，表面亦冇咁容易起「格仔」硬邊。做手機殼嘅曲面，斜紋係合理選擇。

克重（gsm）：一塊布每平方米幾重

克重係每平方米布料嘅重量（g/m²）。呢個係最有實際意義嘅數字，因為佢直接反映單位面積裡面有幾多纖維。一般芳綸布由 100 去到 400 g/m² 都有。克重越高，同樣厚度下纖維越多、越硬、越重。

呢度要講一句老實話：大部分品牌唔會公開自己用幾多層布、總克重幾多。我哋官網寫嘅係厚度（0.65–0.75 毫米）同重量（11–13 克）——因為呢兩個數字你自己用卡尺同電子磅就量得到。布嘅克重、層數、樹脂比例，係工廠內部參數，客人驗唔到，講嚟都係自說自話。我寧願只寫你驗得到嘅嘢。

三、點解佢可以做到 0.7 毫米：剛度係「三次方」嘅遊戲

呢一節係全篇最重要嘅一節。如果你只記得一樣嘢，請記呢句：

一塊板抗彎嘅能力，同佢厚度嘅三次方成正比，同材料本身嘅彈性模數成正比。

寫成公式大概係 D ∝ E × t³（E 係模數，t 係厚度）。呢個「三次方」就係一切嘅源頭。厚度由 1 毫米變 2 毫米，唔係硬一倍，係硬八倍。反過嚟講：如果材料本身夠硬，你就可以大幅減薄而唔損失剛度。

芳綸複合材料嘅模數，同普通塑膠唔係同一個量級。聚碳酸酯（PC）大概 2.3 GPa 級數，TPU 更軟，係 0.0X 至 0.1 GPa 級數；芳綸布壓成嘅層壓板，喺纖維方向上係幾十 GPa 級數。呢個差距，就係「薄一半」嘅物理基礎。

我試過用自己嘅假設計一次，你都可以跟住計（以下係估算，唔係實驗數據）：

芳綸 0.7 mmPC 1.8 mm
密度約 1.4 g/cm³約 1.2 g/cm³
一個約 122 cm² 嘅殼，淨殼料重量約 12 克約 26 克
抗彎剛度（相對）約 2 倍1 倍（基準）

呢個表最反直覺嘅一點係：PC 嘅密度其實比芳綸低。芳綸重過 PC。所以芳綸殼輕，唔係因為材料輕，而係因為佢可以用三分一左右嘅厚度達到同樣甚至更高嘅剛度。「輕」係設計嘅結果，唔係密度嘅結果。如果你聽到有人講「芳綸密度低所以輕」，佢係講錯咗。

呢個數字亦解釋咗官網上嗰句「印刷 PC 假芳綸殼通常 25 克以上」——因為塑膠要做到同樣「唔會軟趴趴」嘅手感，就要加厚到 1.5 至 2 毫米以上，厚度一加，重量就跟住上。

四、薄同輕，對你嚟講究竟有咩分別

「薄」、「輕」呢兩個字好容易被當成 marketing。但佢哋係可以量度、而且每日都感覺到嘅嘢。

口袋

一個 0.7 毫米嘅殼，令手機每邊多 0.7 毫米，即係總共闊 1.4 毫米左右。一個 2.4 毫米嘅防摔殼，係 4.8 毫米。呢 3.4 毫米，就係「入牛仔褲前袋順唔順」同「要唔要用手挖」嘅分別。你做過褲，你就知口袋尺寸係計到好緊嘅。

手感同握法

手機殼嘅握感，唔係單純「滑唔滑」。你隻手係包住一個四邊形，邊框厚度直接改變你手指屈曲嘅角度。厚殼嘅好處係摩擦力大、就算你隻手細都揸得實；薄殼嘅好處係手機回復原本嘅比例，你係揸住部手機，唔係揸住一舊嘢。兩種都有人喜歡，但你唔可以同時要。

重量：12 克 vs 26 克

一部手機大約 170 至 230 克。12 克嘅殼，大約係 5% 至 7%；26 克，大約係 12% 至 15%。你單手舉住部手機睇片二十分鐘，尾指同手腕係會計較呢 14 克嘅。呢個唔係感覺，係力矩。

散熱

呢一點少人講，但真：殼越厚、包得越密，機身熱就越難散。芳綸層壓板傳熱唔算好，但佢只有 0.7 毫米，熱阻細；一個 2.4 毫米嘅複合殼，中間仲有空氣層同軟膠，就係一件保溫衣。夏天打機嘅時候，你會知。

五、佢做唔到嘅事：剛性唔等於吸震

而家講最唔好聽嘅部分。

跌落嘅時候，手機嘅動能一定要有地方去。只有兩條路：一係變成材料嘅形變（吸收），一係傳去手機（傳導）。

橡膠同 TPU 呢類彈性體，受衝擊會大幅變形，分子鏈之間摩擦，將動能變成熱（呢個叫滯後損耗）。佢地係吸收派。芳綸係硬、脆、斷裂伸長率只有 2% 至 4% 級數嘅材料，佢唔會大幅變形，所以佢係傳導派：佢可以做嘅事係將衝擊力分散去更大面積、令手機唔會直接撞地面，但佢唔會幫你「食」咗嗰下撞擊。

所以芳綸殼實際上保護你嘅係：唔會刮花、唔會撞崩邊角、唔會被枱面硬物壓出痕、跌落時手機唔會直接貼地。佢保護唔到嘅係：跌落嘅能量本身。

具體喺邊度失手？

  • 角位直撞硬地。角落係應力集中點，能量擠喺幾平方毫米，剛性材料冇形變空間，力就直接傳去屏幕玻璃。
  • 瓷磚、石屎、階梯邊。呢啲表面唔會讓步，所有能量都要由殼同手機分擔。
  • 屏幕朝下。芳綸殼背板硬，但正面玻璃係最脆弱嘅一面，要靠加高邊框（官網寫 +0.8 毫米鏡頭保護）去換取空間。
  • 連續跌落。複合材料嘅損傷會累積——第一次可能只係內部微裂，第二次就散。

我哋官網嘅做法係喺「不適合」欄裡面直接寫：「每週都會摔手機——請選防摔系列」。呢句唔係免責聲明，係我真心嘅建議。如果你係一年跌三次嘅人，芳綸係完美；如果你係一個月跌三次嘅人，你買芳綸係買錯嘢，我唔想賺你呢筆錢然後聽你投訴。

六、防彈背心同手機殼，隔住幾多個數量級

返到最開頭嗰句。點解我咁肯定唔防彈？

防彈背心（軟式）嘅原理係：好多層芳綸布疊起，子彈打中嘅時候，纖維網把彈頭「兜住」，能量沿住纖維向四方擴散，同時層與層之間滑移、分層，將動能散掉。呢個係一個靠厚度同層數工作嘅系統。業界做 IIIA 級軟甲，要用好多層布，每平方米嘅芳綸重量係好幾公斤級數——唔係幾百克。

而我哋一塊 0.7 毫米嘅層壓板，每平方米芳綸大概係 1 公斤級數。呢個差距唔係「差少少」，係差一個數量級以上；再加埋結構完全唔同（背心係多層滑移網，殼係固化咗嘅硬板），同埋面積（背心包住你整個軀幹，殼只有 122 cm² 左右）。

結論：唔防彈，唔係我哋偷工，係物理。任何一個 0.7 毫米厚嘅手機殼講自己防彈，你唔需要問我，你自己都可以判斷。

七、點分真芳綸同假芳綸：你自己驗得到嘅六個方法

市面上最多嘅「芳綸殼」，係啤塑 PC 殼上面印一層斜紋。呢個唔係秘密，內地產業帶係公開做法。以下係我會用嘅檢查，全部唔需要實驗室。

一、磅重（最有效）

0.7 毫米級嘅真芳綸殼，正常係 11 至 13 克（我哋官網寫 11–13 克）。如果一個聲稱芳綸嘅殼超過 20 克，佢大概唔係芳綸——因為印刷 PC 要做到唔軟，就要加厚。廚房磅都做到呢個判斷。

二、卡尺量厚度

真芳綸殼係 0.65 至 0.75 毫米。量邊位，唔好量鏡頭凸台。卡尺係幾十蚊嘅工具，十分鐘就可以令大部分宣傳現形。

三、用斜光睇織紋

將殼放喺枱燈側邊，慢慢轉角度。真織紋係有深度嘅：光暗會隨角度變化，經緯交叉位有細微高低，表面啞。印刷紋係平嘅：你會見到紋路均勻咁反光、有可能睇到重複嘅印刷網點或條紋，甚至同一個圖案嘅接駁位。呢個測試，用你手機嘅電筒都得。

四、摸同感受溫度

真芳綸摸落微涼、乾爽，有少少似厚紙／布嘅纖維感，唔會即刻變暖。啤塑件摸落光滑、有種「塑膠嘅暖」，好快就同你手溫一樣。呢個係主觀嘅，但配上磅重就唔主觀。

五、睇邊口

芳綸殼係由層壓板 CNC 切割，邊位通常見到極細嘅樹脂／複合邊，有時帶輕微燒焦色同味。啤塑件邊位係圓滑嘅、有分模線、內側可能見到縮水痕。另外，真芳綸殼嘅內側通常都係深色樹脂面，唔會係軟膠內襯再黏一層印花布。

六、問來源同價錢

唔好只問「係唔係芳綸」，問具體啲：布料幾多克重？邊間壓板？用邊種樹脂？厚度同重量幾多？真做得嘅人，答得到。同時，你見到 US$5 嘅「芳綸殼」，唔需要驗——材料成本都未夠。

幾個唔可靠嘅方法（我試過）

  • 拗彎測試。印刷 PC 一樣硬，拗唔出分別。
  • 敲擊聽聲。兩種都係「硬物聲」，我聽唔出，你大概都聽唔出。
  • 摸落有紋就係真。模具可以啤出凹凸紋，摸落有手感同真織紋係兩件事，所以一定要配斜光睇。
  • 用火燒。唔好。真芳綸燒出嚟嘅味同灰燼特徵要專業判斷，你自己燒只會得到一個爛殼。

八、真芳綸貴在邊：一層層拆開成本

我講嘅係成本結構同公開報價嘅大概範圍，唔係某一間廠嘅報價單。

  • 纖維本身。普通塑膠粒（PC、ABS）係每公斤幾美元級數。對位芳綸係特種纖維，每公斤係幾十美元級數，差距係一個數量級。
  • 織布。芳綸唔易織，織機速度慢、損耗高，織成布之後每平方米係幾十美元級數，視乎克重同規格。
  • 預浸／樹脂處理。預浸布（prepreg）要低溫儲存、有保質期，成本再上去一截。
  • 熱壓。塑膠件係注塑，一個循環幾十秒。層壓板係熱壓固化，一個循環係幾十分鐘級數，模具亦要耐高壓。呢個直接決定產能同單件固定成本。
  • CNC 修邊。芳綸切嘅時候會起毛、分層，刀具損耗快；由平板切出殼形，邊角料係廢料，損耗率兩三成係正常範圍。真係做到嘅廠唔多。
  • 批量。呢種料唔可能一次落十萬件。小批量即係每個殼要攤更多開模同設定成本。

講到呢度要停一停，講一句唔幫自己嘅話：一塊殼嘅材料成本，通常只佔零售價嘅一部分，唔係全部。呢個行業嘅價格，主要係由品牌、渠道、工時同故事決定。所以「芳綸貴」唔可以解釋所有價格，佢只係解釋一件事：呢種殼冇可能做到 US$5。你見到 US$5 嘅芳綸，答案已經寫喺價錢牌上。

九、點解唔係碳纖維（呢條問題我答過好多次）

每次有人見到「芳綸」兩個字，下一句通常係：「點解唔用碳纖？碳纖唔係高級啲咩？」

呢條問題值得認真答，因為答案唔係「碳纖貴啲」，而係碳纖會令我哋做唔到某啲功能。

單睇力學，碳纖係贏嘅：模數可以高到 200 GPa 以上（芳綸係幾十至一百 GPa 級數），密度 1.5 至 1.8 g/cm³ 左右，同芳綸差唔多，抗壓亦遠好過芳綸（芳綸嘅死穴）。如果我只係想一塊「最硬最薄」嘅板，碳纖係更正確嘅答案。

但碳纖有一個物理特性，令佢喺手機殼上有硬傷：碳纖導電。

  • 無線充電。無線充電係靠交變磁場工作。導電體放喺交變磁場裡面會產生渦流——一部分能量變咗熱，充電效率下降，嚴重嘅會觸發過熱保護。呢個唔係「可能」，係法拉第電磁感應。
  • 訊號同 NFC。導電材料會屏蔽電磁場。旗艦手機嘅 NFC 天線同無線充電線圈通常喺背面上方，一塊導電背板就係一塊屏蔽板。
  • 斷裂模式。碳纖斷裂係脆性斷裂，會產生導電嘅細碎屑。呢樣嘢對於一部有金屬中框、有裸露接點嘅手機，係一個我唔想承擔嘅風險。
  • 外觀。碳纖基本上只有黑色（深灰），而且織紋好容易變成「賽車感」。想要啞色、暖調、低調，做法有限。
  • 加工安全。碳纖粉塵係導電粉塵，對車間電氣設備同工人呼吸道都有風險，防護要求高過芳綸。

我哋官網材質頁寫嘅一句，就係呢個考量嘅結論：「芳綸與 PC 不會阻擋磁場，厚的金屬片才會。」呢句話亦解釋咗點解碳纖同金屬都唔喺我哋嘅選項裡面。

一句話：碳纖係「力學上更好，功能上更差」。手機殼唔係一級方程式尾翼，佢要同時做一件事——唔好干擾部手機。呢個就係我唔揀碳纖嘅唯一理由，亦係足夠嘅理由。

十、呢塊板會唔會老化：紫外線、邊口、清潔

複合材料唔會「爛」，但會老化。你買之前應該知道佢點老。

  • 紫外線。芳綸纖維本身怕 UV，但佢被封喺樹脂裡面，所以真正曝露喺 UV 面前嘅係樹脂。長期直射陽光，樹脂（尤其淺色環氧）會慢慢黃化。深色（石墨黑、午夜藍）最唔顯。呢個係「幾年之後微微變色」，唔係「半年就爛」。
  • 邊口。0.7 毫米薄板嘅邊位係最脆弱嘅地方。撞落硬物、經常同鎖匙放同一個袋，邊口會崩、會分層。呢個係物理，唔係做工問題。
  • 清潔。官網保養指示係「用微濕布擦拭，避免溶劑與研磨性清潔劑」。呢句有原因：酒精、天拿水、去污膏會令樹脂表面霧化，啞面一旦被溶劑咬花就冇得救。油污用微濕布加一滴中性洗手液就夠。
  • 唔好當佢係軟殼。唔好拗、唔好撳、唔好嘗試套落唔對應嘅機型。層壓板係結構件，唔係彈性體；硬套會令邊位分層。
  • 溫度。唔好長期放喺車頭。唔係會熔（佢 500 °C 以上才分解），而係樹脂長期受熱會加速老化。

所以我唔會講「一個殼用十年」。正常使用，兩三年之後你會見到邊口有輕微磨損、顏色可能微變——但唔會影響功能。呢個係一件結構件應有嘅老化曲線，唔係一件耗材。

十一、我點解最後仍然揀佢

講完咁多限制，你可能以為我唔鍾意芳綸。相反，我認為佢係三個系列裡面技術含量最高嘅一個，原因好簡單：喺常見殼料裡面，佢係唯一可以同時做到薄、輕、硬嘅。

要薄，你唔可以用軟膠（軟膠薄就冇保護）。要硬，你唔可以用純塑膠（塑膠要厚才有剛度，厚就重）。要輕，你唔可以用金屬（金屬剛度好，但密度係 2.7 以上，而且會擋 MagSafe 磁場同無線充電）。芳綸啱啱好落喺呢三條線嘅交叉點上。

仲有一點：芳綸唔導電、唔隔磁。呢個好重要，因為我哋三個系列都要放 N52 釹磁石陣列（對齊充電線圈）。金屬背板會直接廢掉 MagSafe；芳綸同 PC 都唔會。呢個係材料選擇嘅硬約束，唔係偏好。

所以如果你問我「芳綸殼啱邊種人」，我會咁答：

  • 啱：想要最薄、最接近裸機手感；手機長期放褲袋或者西裝內袋；主要怕刮花同枱面磨損；一年跌一兩次；討厭厚重同假皮。
  • 唔啱：每星期都跌；做戶外或者工地；成日唔記得放好部手機；想「一次買殼保三年」。呢啲人應該買防摔系列，唔係買芳綸。

我投資嘅 HANDSOME 三個系列，芳綸係我會推薦畀「知道自己要乜」嘅人嘅那一個，唔多講。

十二、三句話總結

第一句：芳綸係一種性格好鮮明嘅材料——順住纖維方向好強，受壓、受衝擊、見紫外線就差。用佢就係用佢嘅長處：薄、輕、剛。

第二句：佢唔防彈、唔吸震，呢兩點係物理事實，唔係謙虛。跌手機係防摔系列嘅工作。

第三句：你自己驗得到嘅數字（克重同毫米）比任何形容詞可靠。真芳綸殼 11 至 13 克、0.65 至 0.75 毫米。超過 20 克，你可以收手。

做生意二十六年，我最怕嘅唔係客人話貴，係客人買完之後覺得被誤導。材料呢件事，我寧願講到悶，都唔想講到靚。

We sell aramid cases, so the first thing I have to say is something that loses us customers: this case will not stop a bullet.

That is not modesty, and it is not the legal department talking. It is because the word "aramid" goes straight to "bulletproof vest" in most people's heads. If I don't say this in the first paragraph, sooner or later you will feel you were misled.

I came up through factories — electronics, appliances, hardware, twenty-six years. I am not a materials scientist. But I have one habit. Whenever someone tells me a material is amazing, I ask three questions: How was that number measured? Under what conditions does it hold? When does it stop holding? Aramid is the material I have asked about most, because it is the one most people oversell.

This piece goes from the molecule to the cost. I'll explain why it is the thinnest option, and where it fails. All of it is what actually goes through my head when I choose a material. You can disagree with me, but you won't catch me getting the numbers wrong.

1. What aramid actually is: a molecule that refuses to bend

"Aramid" is short for aromatic polyamide. The name is dull, but take it apart and it makes sense. Aromatic means the backbone is built from benzene rings — stiff, hard to twist. Polyamide means the rings are strung together by amide bonds (-CO-NH-), which puts it in the same family as nylon. The difference is that a nylon chain folds and coils like cooked spaghetti, while an aramid chain is a rigid rod.

That sounds academic, but it is where everything starts. What goes into a case is para-aramid: the rings and amide bonds line up in a straight line, one after another. The chemical name is poly(p-phenylene terephthalamide), PPTA. Kevlar and Twaron are both this. There is also meta-aramid (Nomex, for example), where the chain kinks sideways. It is not stiff, but it resists heat and flame — the stuff in firefighter gear. Two very different personalities, both called aramid. Don't mix them up.

The manufacturing is unusual too. Para-aramid won't dissolve in ordinary solvents, so it is dissolved in concentrated sulfuric acid to make a liquid-crystal solution — meaning the chains are already largely aligned before the fibre even exists. That solution is extruded through a spinneret, passes through a short air gap (this is called dry-jet wet spinning), and then into a coagulation bath. The air gap matters: the fibre is stretched, and the polymer chains straighten and align along the fibre axis. The result: pull along the fibre and it is superb; push sideways or in compression and it is poor.

On numbers, I'll give public ranges rather than pretend to false precision. Para-aramid density is around 1.44 g/cm³. Tensile strength is in the 3 GPa class — in textile terms, over 20 grams of load per denier. Modulus (how stubborn it is) is roughly 70 to 110 GPa depending on grade. DuPont's famous line for Kevlar was that, weight for weight, it is about five times stronger than steel. That is their comparison, not my invention, and I'm quoting it as theirs.

But the same datasheets have a few lines in small print that most people skip:

  • Compressive strength is far below tensile strength. Pull it and it is strong; compress it and the fibres kink. Aramid is a tension material, not a compression material.
  • UV light yellows it and costs it strength. It has to be sealed inside resin. It cannot be left bare.
  • It absorbs moisture — a few percent. Not much, but enough that both faces need to be sealed with resin.
  • It does not melt. Above roughly 500 °C it decomposes rather than turning into a puddle.
  • It bonds poorly to resin unless the fabric is surface-treated first, which adds cost.

By now you should see it: aramid is not a superhero material. It is a material with a very distinct personality — it can hold a tonne in one direction and is ordinary in the other. If you use it, you accept its temper, instead of sticking a "premium" label on it.

2. From a bundle of yarn to a case: denier, weave, areal weight

People assume an aramid case is fibre poured into a mould. It isn't. The real sequence is: fibre spun into yarn → yarn woven into fabric → fabric impregnated with resin (or bought as prepreg) → layers stacked → hot-pressed and cured → CNC-trimmed and drilled. Every step shows up in the thing in your hand.

Denier: how thick a bundle of yarn is

Denier is a basic textile unit, and the definition is simple: the weight in grams of 9,000 metres of the yarn. 1,500D means 9,000 metres weighs 1,500 grams. Bigger number, thicker yarn.

What does it change for you? Three things:

  • How visible the weave is. Coarser yarn (1,500D, say) gives a weave with depth and weight to it — it reads as "thick material". Finer yarn (600D, say) gives a tight, smooth surface that looks almost like a plain panel from a distance.
  • Feel. Fine yarn feels refined; coarse yarn has a clear fibre relief — what people call a dry, fibrous hand.
  • Abrasion resistance and stiffness. In the same weave, coarser yarn means more fibre per unit area: better abrasion resistance, stiffer, but also heavier and thicker.

This industry mostly uses 1000D and 1500D. I won't tell you 1500D is simply better than 1000D, because it's a trade-off: a bolder weave means slightly more weight and a surface with more relief; a finer weave means giving up some abrasion resistance and stiffness. Nothing is free.

What "3K twill" means — and the fact that it's borrowed vocabulary

"K" is the number of filaments in a bundle: 3K means 3,000 filaments in one tow. That vocabulary belongs to carbon fibre, where 3K and 12K are standard. Aramid has traditionally been specified by denier, not by K. So when you see "3K twill weave" on an aramid case, strictly speaking it is carbon-fibre vocabulary borrowed to describe a surface texture. It isn't wrong, but you should know it is not a specification you can take to a lab. It describes an appearance.

"Twill" means the yarn floats over two and under two, creating a diagonal, stair-stepped pattern — the kind that catches light on the diagonal. Compared with plain weave (one over, one under), twill is softer and drapes better, so it wraps a curved surface more cleanly and is less prone to hard grid edges. For the curves on a phone case, twill is the sensible choice.

Areal weight (gsm): how much a square metre of fabric weighs

Areal weight is grams per square metre (g/m²). It is the most meaningful number of the three, because it tells you how much fibre sits in a given area. Aramid fabrics run from about 100 to 400 g/m². Higher areal weight means more fibre for the same thickness: stiffer and heavier.

One honest note here. Most brands won't publish how many layers they use or the total areal weight. We publish thickness (0.65–0.75 mm) and weight (11–13 g), because you can check both yourself with calipers and a kitchen scale. Areal weight, layer count and resin ratio are internal factory parameters. A customer cannot verify them, and quoting them is just talking to hear yourself. I would rather publish only what you can measure.

3. Why it can be 0.7 mm: stiffness is a cube game

This is the most important section in the article. If you remember one thing, remember this:

A panel's resistance to bending scales with the cube of its thickness, and in proportion to the material's elastic modulus.

As a formula, roughly D ∝ E × t³ (E is modulus, t is thickness). That cube is the whole story. Going from 1 mm to 2 mm doesn't make it twice as stiff — it makes it eight times stiffer. Which also means: if the material itself is stiff enough, you can take a lot of thickness out without losing rigidity.

Aramid laminate and ordinary plastics are not on the same scale. Polycarbonate is around 2.3 GPa; TPU is far softer, in the 0.0X to 0.1 GPa range. A pressed aramid laminate runs in the tens of GPa along the fibre direction. That gap is the physical basis for "half the thickness".

I ran the arithmetic once on my own assumptions. You can follow it (this is an estimate, not test data):

Aramid 0.7 mmPC 1.8 mm
Densityabout 1.4 g/cm³about 1.2 g/cm³
Shell material for a ~122 cm² caseabout 12 gabout 26 g
Flexural stiffness (relative)about 2×1× (baseline)

The counter-intuitive part of that table is this: PC is actually less dense than aramid. Aramid is the heavier material. So an aramid case is light not because the material is light, but because it needs about a third of the thickness to reach the same or better stiffness. Light is a design outcome, not a density outcome. If someone tells you "aramid is light because its density is low", they have it backwards.

The same numbers explain the line on our own site — that a printed "aramid" PC case usually weighs 25 g or more. Plastic has to be thickened to 1.5–2 mm before it stops feeling floppy, and thickness brings weight with it.

4. What thin and light actually change for you

"Thin" and "light" are easy to dismiss as marketing. But they are measurable, and you feel them daily.

Pockets

A 0.7 mm case adds 0.7 mm to each side of the phone — about 1.4 mm of width in total. A 2.4 mm protective case adds 4.8 mm. Those 3.4 mm are the difference between sliding the phone into a jeans pocket and having to dig it out. I have made trousers. Pocket dimensions are cut tight on purpose.

Feel and grip

How a case grips is not just about slipperiness. Your hand wraps a rectangle, and the thickness of the frame changes the angle your fingers curl at. A thick case gives more friction, so even a small hand feels secure. A thin case gives you back the phone's original proportions — you are holding a phone, not a lump. People like both. You can't have both at once.

Weight: 12 g versus 26 g

A phone weighs about 170–230 g. A 12 g case is roughly 5–7% of that; 26 g is 12–15%. If you hold a phone one-handed watching video for twenty minutes, your little finger and wrist do notice the extra 14 g. That is not a feeling, it is a moment arm.

Heat

Few people mention this, but it's real. The thicker and more enclosed the case, the harder it is for the phone to shed heat. Aramid laminate is not a great conductor, but at 0.7 mm the thermal resistance is small. A 2.4 mm composite case with foam and soft rubber inside is a jacket. You will find out on a summer gaming session.

5. What it cannot do: stiff is not the same as shock-absorbing

Now the unpleasant part.

When a phone falls, the kinetic energy has to go somewhere. There are only two routes: it becomes deformation in the material (absorbed), or it travels into the phone (transmitted).

Elastomers like rubber and TPU deform a lot under impact, and the internal friction between polymer chains turns kinetic energy into heat — hysteresis. They are the absorbers. Aramid is stiff and brittle, with elongation at break in the 2–4% class. It barely deforms, so it is a transmitter: what it can do is spread the impact over a larger area and stop the phone touching the ground. It will not eat the impact for you.

So what an aramid case really protects is: scratches, chipped corners, marks from hard objects on a desk, and the phone coming into direct contact with the floor. What it does not protect is the energy of the fall itself.

Where does it fail, concretely?

  • A corner hitting hard ground. Corners concentrate stress; the energy is squeezed into a few square millimetres; a rigid material has no room to deform, so the force goes straight into the glass.
  • Tile, concrete, stair edges. These surfaces don't give, so the energy has to be shared between the case and the phone.
  • Face-down drops. The back panel is rigid, but the front glass is the fragile side. That's what the raised lip buys you (our site lists +0.8 mm of camera protection).
  • Repeated drops. Composite damage accumulates — the first fall may only micro-crack internally; the second one finishes the job.

On our own site, under "not for", we write plainly: "You drop your phone weekly — take the Shield instead." That is not a disclaimer. It is what I actually think. If you drop your phone three times a year, aramid is perfect. If you drop it three times a month, buying aramid is buying the wrong thing — and I don't want to take that money and then hear the complaint.

6. How many orders of magnitude separate a vest from a phone case

Back to the first line. Why am I so certain it won't stop a bullet?

A soft ballistic vest works by stacking many layers of aramid fabric. When a round hits, the fibre network catches it and spreads the energy outward along the fibres, while the layers slip and delaminate against each other, dissipating the round's energy. It is a system that works through thickness and layer count. A commercial level IIIA soft panel uses many layers, and the aramid in it weighs several kilograms per square metre — not hundreds of grams.

Our 0.7 mm laminate carries roughly a kilogram of aramid per square metre. That is not a small difference; it is more than an order of magnitude. Add to that a completely different structure (a vest is a sliding multi-layer net, a case is a cured rigid plate), and a completely different area (a vest covers a torso, a case covers about 122 cm²).

Conclusion: it won't stop a bullet, not because we cut corners, but because of physics. Any 0.7 mm phone case claiming to be bulletproof doesn't need my opinion. You can work it out yourself.

7. Telling real aramid from fake: six checks you can run yourself

The most common "aramid case" on the market is a moulded PC shell with a twill pattern printed on it. That is not a secret; in the Chinese supply belt it is standard practice. Here is what I check. None of it needs a lab.

One: weigh it (the most effective)

A genuine 0.7 mm aramid case is normally 11 to 13 grams (our site says 11–13 g). If a case claiming to be aramid is over 20 g, it probably isn't aramid — because a printed PC shell has to be thickened before it stops feeling soft. A kitchen scale is enough to make this call.

Two: measure the thickness

Real aramid cases are 0.65 to 0.75 mm. Measure the side wall, not the camera bump. Calipers cost very little, and ten minutes with them will expose most marketing claims.

Three: look at the weave in raking light

Put the case beside a desk lamp and rotate it slowly. A real weave has depth: the light changes with the angle, the weft/weft crossover points have subtle relief, and the surface is matte. A printed pattern is flat: it reflects evenly, you may see repeating print dots or banding, and sometimes a seam where the pattern tiles. Your phone's torch works for this.

Four: feel it, and feel its temperature

Real aramid feels cool and dry, a little like thick paper or cloth, and it doesn't warm up immediately. A moulded part feels smooth and "plastic-warm", and matches your hand temperature quickly. This one is subjective — but combined with the weight, it isn't.

Five: look at the cut edge

Aramid cases are CNC-cut from laminate, so the edge usually shows a fine resin/composite line, sometimes with slight burn discolouration and smell. A moulded part has rounded edges, a parting line, and possibly sink marks inside. A real aramid case also usually has a dark resin face inside — not a soft liner glued to a printed fabric.

Six: ask about the source, and about the price

Don't just ask "is it aramid?" Ask specifically: What is the fabric's areal weight? Who pressed the laminate? What resin? What thickness and weight? Someone who genuinely makes it can answer. And if you see an "aramid case" at US$5, you don't need to test anything — the material cost alone doesn't fit.

Methods that don't work (I've tried them)

  • The bend test. Printed PC is stiff too. No difference to be found.
  • Tapping it and listening. Both sound like hard objects. I can't hear it; you probably can't either.
  • "If it has texture, it's real." Moulds can emboss a relief pattern. Texture under a thumb and a real weave are two different things, which is why you always pair this with raking light.
  • Burning it. Don't. Reading aramid's burn smell and ash needs training, and all you'll get is a ruined case.

8. Where the money goes: taking the cost apart

I'm describing the cost structure and rough public price ranges, not one factory's quotation.

  • The fibre itself. Ordinary plastic pellets (PC, ABS) cost a few dollars per kilogram. Para-aramid is a specialty fibre in the tens of dollars per kilogram. An order of magnitude apart.
  • Weaving. Aramid is not easy to weave: slower looms, more waste. Woven fabric lands in the tens of dollars per square metre, depending on areal weight and grade.
  • Impregnation and resin. Prepreg needs cold storage and has a shelf life, which adds another step in cost.
  • Hot pressing. Plastic parts are injection moulded: a cycle takes tens of seconds. Laminate is press-cured: a cycle takes tens of minutes, and the tooling must take high pressure. That directly sets capacity and fixed cost per unit.
  • CNC trimming. Aramid fuzzes and delaminates when cut, and eats tooling. Cutting a case shape out of a flat panel leaves offcuts as waste — a 20–30% loss rate is normal. Not many factories will actually take this on.
  • Batch size. This material does not allow a 100,000-piece run. Small batches mean every case carries more tooling and setup cost.

One pause here, and this part doesn't help me sell: the material cost of a case is usually only part of the retail price, not all of it. Pricing in this industry is driven mainly by brand, channel, labour and story. So "aramid is expensive" does not explain every price tag. It explains exactly one thing: this kind of case cannot be made for US$5. When you see a US$5 aramid case, the answer is already on the price tag.

9. Why not carbon fibre (I get asked this constantly)

Every time someone sees the word "aramid", the next line is usually: "Why not carbon fibre? Isn't carbon more premium?"

That deserves a proper answer, because the answer is not "carbon is more expensive". It is that carbon fibre would stop us delivering certain functions.

On pure mechanics, carbon wins: modulus can exceed 200 GPa (aramid runs in the tens to about a hundred GPa), density is around 1.5–1.8 g/cm³, similar to aramid, and its compressive behaviour is far better — aramid's weak point. If all I wanted was the stiffest, thinnest panel, carbon would be the better answer.

But carbon has one physical property that is a hard defect in a phone case: carbon fibre conducts electricity.

  • Wireless charging. Wireless charging works through an alternating magnetic field. A conductor sitting in an alternating field generates eddy currents — some of the energy becomes heat, charging efficiency drops, and in bad cases the phone hits thermal protection. This is not "possible". It is Faraday induction.
  • Signal and NFC. Conductive materials shield electromagnetic fields. The NFC antenna and the charging coil in most flagship phones sit near the back. A conductive back panel is a shield.
  • Failure mode. Carbon fails in a brittle way and produces conductive fragments. On a phone with a metal frame and exposed contacts, that is a risk I don't want to take.
  • Looks. Carbon is essentially only black (dark grey), and the weave reads as "motorsport" very quickly. If you want matte, warm and quiet, there isn't much room.
  • Machining safety. Carbon dust is conductive dust — a risk to workshop electrical equipment and to workers' lungs. It needs stricter protection than aramid.

The line on our own materials page is the conclusion of that thinking: "Aramid and polycarbonate do not block the field — thick metal plates do." The same sentence explains why carbon fibre and metal are not on our list either.

In one line: carbon is mechanically better and functionally worse. A phone case is not a Formula One wing. It has to do one more job — stay out of the phone's way. That is the whole reason I don't use carbon, and it is reason enough.

10. Will the panel age? UV, edges, cleaning

Composites don't rot, but they do age. You should know how before you buy.

  • UV. The aramid fibre itself dislikes UV, but it is sealed in resin, so what actually faces the sun is the resin. Prolonged direct sun slowly yellows resin, especially light-coloured epoxy. Dark finishes (graphite, midnight) hide it best. This is "slightly different colour after a few years", not "ruined in six months".
  • The cut edge. On a 0.7 mm panel the edge is the most vulnerable part. Knock it against something hard, or keep it in the same pocket as your keys, and the edge will chip and delaminate. That is physics, not workmanship.
  • Cleaning. Our care note says to wipe with a damp cloth and avoid solvents and abrasive cleaners. There is a reason: alcohol, thinners and scouring paste fog the resin surface, and once a matte finish is bitten by solvent it doesn't come back. A damp cloth and a drop of mild hand soap handles grease.
  • Don't treat it like a rubber case. Don't bend it, don't flex it, don't force it onto a model it wasn't made for. Laminate is a structural part, not an elastomer; forcing it delaminates the edge.
  • Heat. Don't leave it on a dashboard long-term. It won't melt (it decomposes above 500 °C), but sustained heat accelerates resin ageing.

So I won't claim "one case for ten years". With normal use, after two or three years you will see slight edge wear and maybe a subtle colour shift — without any loss of function. That is the ageing curve of a structural part, not of a consumable.

11. Why I still choose it

After all those limits, you might think I don't like aramid. The opposite. I think it is the most technically interesting of our three ranges, for a simple reason: among common case materials, it is the only one that can be thin, light and rigid at the same time.

To be thin you can't use soft rubber — thin rubber protects nothing. To be rigid you can't use plain plastic — plastic needs thickness for stiffness, and thickness is weight. To be light you can't use metal — metal is stiff, but its density is above 2.7 and it blocks MagSafe and wireless charging. Aramid sits exactly where those three lines cross.

One more thing: aramid neither conducts nor shields magnetism. That matters, because all three of our ranges carry an N52 neodymium magnet array aligned to the charging coil. A metal back plate kills MagSafe outright; aramid and PC don't. That is a hard constraint on material choice, not a preference.

So if you ask me who an aramid case is for:

  • For you if: you want the thinnest possible fit and near-bare-phone feel; the phone lives in a trouser or jacket pocket; your main worry is scratches and desk wear; you drop it once or twice a year; you dislike bulk and fake leather.
  • Not for you if: you drop it weekly; you work outdoors or on sites; you regularly forget where you put the phone; you want one case to last three years. Those people should buy the drop-protection range, not aramid.

Of the three ranges at HANDSOME, the brand I invested in, aramid is the one I'd recommend to people who already know what they want. I won't go on about it.

12. Three sentences to finish

One: Aramid has a very clear personality — superb along the fibre, poor in compression, under impact, and in UV. Using it means using its strengths: thin, light, rigid.

Two: It is not bulletproof and it does not absorb shock. Both are physics, not modesty. Dropping phones is the drop-protection range's job.

Three: The numbers you can check yourself (grams and millimetres) beat any adjective. Real aramid: 11 to 13 grams, 0.65 to 0.75 mm. Over 20 grams, put it down.

Twenty-six years in business, and what I fear is not a customer saying it's expensive. It is a customer feeling misled afterwards. On materials I would rather be boring than flattering.
